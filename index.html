What is negotiation?

Ppl hardwired to assume that things in life are zero sum games. Therefore see negotiation as figuring out how to distribute something. Trying to claim as much of the pie as you can, trying to beat the other person looks like doing as much as you can.

BUT: most of life is integrative, size of pie changes by how you slice it, how you negotiate can make it positive sum.

Distributive bargaining. Win-lose split of a fixed resource.

Integrative negotiation. Value creations and joint optimization.

Variable-sum games. Size of the pie in terms of utility changes depending on how it’s sliced.

Zero-sum game. Assumption: fixed pie, total value is the same regardless of agreement terms. Assumption: gain for party 1 is an equal loss for party 2.

The role of expectations, goals, first offers, and concession patterns in negotiation.

First offers / anchoring. The first number gets too much weight. A landlord’s opening lease terms pull the tenant’s counteroffer toward that frame. Set your position before you hear theirs.

Expectations / aspirations. The outcome you actually want. It has to be better than the walk-away price.

Goals (the slides label this OBJ). A specific outcome you want.

  1. Specific
  2. difficult
  3. commit to them.

Self-efficacy is believing you can get it. Commitment is keeping it when they push.

The opening. An extreme first offer anchors them. Too extreme, and they walk. Open firm, then make the first concessions tiny, and you signal toughness. Open firm and then make real concessions, and you signal flexibility.

Concession pattern. Decide in advance how you move from the opening toward the target.

  • Shrinking steps say you are near your limit. Equal steps do not. Your example is Mario’s $4, $4, $4.
  • A concession that is not returned looks like weakness. Match the size.
  • Package the moves. “If you move on A and B, I’ll move on C and D” beats handing them over one at a time.
  • Call it the final offer and then concede again, and they read the pattern as broken.
  • People who care about the other side concede more. Toughness gets the more favorable outcome.
  • People underestimate how far the other side would have gone.

Why the pattern works on you. Giving a concession is a reward. Law of effect: behavior that gets a plus is more likely to come back. Counterfactual thinking, which you already had under equity theory: you do not react to the dollar amount. You react to the deal you can imagine having gotten instead. The pattern makes that other deal easy or hard to picture. Firm words and firm actions make the better alternative harder to picture.

Make the first offer if you are prepared. The first number becomes the point both of you adjust from. People do not adjust enough, so the person who sets it pulls the settlement toward themselves. An ambitious opening wins:

  1. it leaves you room to move while you learn what they care about, and it
  2. makes them feel there is a long way to go, so they concede more than they planned and start doubting their own limit.

You need the BATNA and the plan worked out before you do this.

Bilateral, not unilateral. Both sides move, and the moves are of similar weight. You move and they do not, and you look weak. The line you wrote: their move is not enough, and you will not move again until they concede something specific. Tie your move to theirs.

Timing. A concession that arrives late says the same thing as a small one: little room remains. A concession they had to wait and work for feels more valuable than the same concession handed over immediately. People are less satisfied when their first offer is simply accepted, because they imagine they could have done better. The pattern you want is slow and shrinking, and only after they have moved too.

Goals-setting theory (Locke and Latham) applies to the target, not the floor.

Integrative - variable sum negotiation.

Integrative negotiation. Value creations and joint optimization. Moving from zero-sum tactics to Pareto Efficient Frontier + sustainable leadership.

Conflict happens. Two sisters could both want the same orange. One wants the peel, and the other wants the fruit, but they negotiate poorly, so they cut it in half, and neither gets fully what they want. Cutting things down the middle in conflict is dumb + ineffective.

Variable-sum games. Size of the pie in terms of utility changes depending on how it’s sliced. Assumption: we have different utility values for different things. This is most real world situations. Ie. a gift that i don’t want but someone gave me bc they’d want it. Ie. dishes and taking out the trash.

Pareto Efficient Frontier. Points where no party can be made better off without making another party worse off. Moving from sub-Pareto-optimal points to the frontier curve is expanding the pie. Most ppl in our class did not reach the Pareto Efficient Frontier – maybe many negotiations do not.

Negotiation 2: job offer, 5 issues: salary, vacation, annual raise, start date, medical coverage. Partner: Binny Milstein. More dimensions so felt more integrative, could trade as we went along. Saved days off as a small concession for the end so felt positive to them.

If you split each down the middle, got very low points. If you traded heavily then had more points.

If negotiation were zero-sum, then the dots would be sloping down. As one side gained more, the other side would be getting less. Key: we each had different point values assigned to each category, salary was less important to me but medical coverage/raise was more – so i could have given them on salary and gotten more on medical coverage for a good trade.

Learning: by figuring out what is important to someone you can both win in a negotiation by trying to meet each person's individual needs.

Factors affecting discovering and integrating interests.

Strategy for a variable-sum game.

  1. Uncover root interests
    Dig beneath positions to find the underlying needs
  2. Expand issues
    Identify every separate issue
  3. Leverage differences
    Capitalize on the asymptomatic utilities(priorities/beliefs/risk) assigned to diff things to move towards Pareto Frontier

Strategies here:

  • Interest analysis. Logically map the why.
  • Inquiry on priorities. Ask targeted questions.
  • Package deals. Trade multiple items at once to find pareto improvement.
  • Perspective taking. Try to understand their point of view.

Why negotiators conceal interests.

  1. good intentions
    Protect org information , personal dignity(bc true reason is embarrassing to say : ie. many ppl think it’s bad to want money so uncomfortable saying that)
  2. strategic advantage
    Fear that giving away preference will lead to counterparty exploiting
  3. information asymmetry
    Want baseline trust , want reciprocated sharing of information (needs to be a dance)

The three levels of culture (Schein’s iceberg model) Artifacts, Values (espoused and in-use) and assumptions

Your iceberg note: the tip you can see is artifacts and behaviors. Under that are attitudes and cognitions about the work, about people, and about who belongs. You have to deduce the attitude from the action, because the person may not have it neat, or may misunderstand their own cognition.

The Culture II slide names the three levels in the review sheet’s words.

  • Artifacts. Concrete observables, including behaviors. The Disney ride operator always smiles. Someone is kicked for calling the person in the boss’s chair an idiot.
  • Values, espoused and in-use. Espoused is what is said: the customer is a guest, so treat them well; we treat each other with respect. In-use is the value the behavior is actually following. Your older example of the split still stands: the line that every employee is cared about, and then a separate cafeteria and a chef for the three top executives.
  • Underlying assumptions. Taken-for-granted cognitions. Disney is a park where the customer is treated well and the experience is good. In the boiler room, the assumption is that the person wants to be filthy rich.

When the spoken value and the behavior match, the culture is what it claims. When they do not, the sentence is the middle of the iceberg and the behavior is the value.

Describe how concession patterns can be used as information signals. How could you use this information to make sense of the other person’s concession patterns and how can you use this in making your own concessions?

A concession is a signal, not just a price cut. You react to the deal you can imagine having gotten instead, and the pattern is what makes that other deal easy or hard to picture.

How to read theirs.

  • Shrinking steps: they are near their resistance point. This deal is close to the best one available. You come away satisfied, and you read them as bargaining in good faith.
  • Equal steps, Mario’s $4, $4, $4: you cannot tell they are near the limit.
  • They accept your first number immediately, or the concessions are large: you can picture a better deal you did not get. Firm words and firm actions make that picture harder.
  • They say it is final and then concede again: the pattern was broken, and the “final” loses its meaning.
  • A concession you do not return looks like weakness to them. They will keep pushing.

How to make your own.

  • Decide the pattern before you sit down. Move from the opening toward the target on purpose.
  • Taper. Shrinking steps tell them you are close to your BATNA.

Why is it useful to know your own and the other party’s walk-away points?

The two walk-away points are the edges of the bargaining range. Overlap means a deal exists in this snapshot. No overlap means there is no deal now, and there might be one later.

Yours stops you from signing something worse than the alternative you already have. Theirs tells you how far you can push before they leave, which is the number people tend to underestimate.

Knowing theirs is also a tool. You can test whether their alternative is as good as they think, and you can try to worsen it. Barry’s mishap sits on the other side of this: if you push them past the point where they feel they lost, they may sabotage the deal later. If you will deal with them again, you bargain less hard because of that.

Do not say yours out loud.

  1. The relationship reason. Saying “here is the least I will take, and here is what I will do if you don’t meet it” tells them the relationship is a price. Most people, including in a job, want to feel it is more than a transaction. Stating your walk-away number makes you look ready to leave for a dollar, and they stop treating you as someone they might give a better deal.
  2. makes it easy for them to just offer that and not anything higher

How should you set your walk-away point?

Set it from the BATNA, not from a number that feels tough.

  1. Brainstorm alternatives, as many as possible
  2. Evaluate each alternative
  3. At this snapshot in time, your best alternative is your RP
  4. Improve it

The aspiration sits above that floor. It is the outcome you actually want, and it has to be better than the walk-away. The goal is that outcome.

  1. Specific
  2. difficult
  3. commit to them.

Recompute it when the snapshot changes. A range that is closed today can be open later, and an alternative you have improved is a new walk-away.

The goal you set is the target, not this floor. Locke and Latham:

  1. Specific
  2. difficult
  3. commit to them.

It should be difficult:

  2. creates the concession pattern that leads to the feeling that things were worked out thru the negotiation
